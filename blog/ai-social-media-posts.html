Can AI Write My Business’s Social Media Posts Without Embarrassing Me?

📅 October 22, 2026 ⏱️ 8 min read ✍️ Oleg Lychkovski

Short answer: yes, if two things are true. It writes only from facts you approved, and something checks every post before it goes out. AI that writes from memory guesses: a price you never charged, a service you don’t offer, a date that never happened. AI that writes from your own facts, with a check in front of the publish button, produces the posts you would have written yourself on a good day.

Embarrassment rarely comes from AI sounding robotic. It comes from a post that says something untrue about your business. That is a process problem, and it has a process answer.

The Four Things That Go Wrong, and How to Fix Each One

Owners who have tried an AI tool tend to have the same four worries. Each one is real. Each one also has a specific fix, and none of the fixes is “write a better prompt.”

1. It makes things up

Why it happens. A general AI tool predicts what a believable post sounds like. Ask it for a post about your spring special and it may add “starting at $99” or “same-day service,” because posts like that usually say so. It does not know your prices; it knows what prices usually look like. The sentence reads well, so nobody catches it until a customer quotes it back to you.

What fixes it.

  • A facts file it must write from. One page: your services, the prices you are happy to publish, the areas you serve, what you do not do, your hours. If it is not in the file, it does not go in a post.
  • A check on every number and name. Before anything is scheduled, each price, date, figure and name in the draft is matched against the facts file or a source. Anything that does not match is cut, or the post is held for you.
  • No promises without you. Offers, guarantees and discounts never start with the system. They start with you.

In practice: this is how our own publishing works. Anything the system cannot verify is held back and shown to a person instead of being sent.

2. It sounds like everyone else

Why it happens. Given nothing specific, AI writes the average post: “5 tips for…”, “Here’s why it matters.” Nothing in it is wrong, and nothing in it is yours. Generic posts are not embarrassing, but they are invisible, and a page full of them looks like nobody is home.

What fixes it.

  • Feed it real questions. The questions customers ask you by phone, by text and in reviews are the best topics you have. Keep a running list and add to it every week.
  • Start from something you wrote. We write a blog article first and turn it into the posts for each channel, so every post carries a real point of view and links back to the full answer.
  • Show it your voice. Three posts you were happy with, and a short list of words you never use, teach it more than any instruction.
  • Use your own photos of your work and your place whenever you have them.

In practice: a post that answers a question your customers asked this month is never generic, whoever typed it.

3. It repeats itself

Why it happens. Most tools start every post from a blank page. They do not remember that the same tip went out two weeks ago, so it comes back, and then again. Your followers notice before you do.

What fixes it.

  • A log of everything published: the date, the channel, the text and the link, in one place.
  • A look back before each draft. New posts are compared with what went out on the same channel in the last three weeks. Anything too close is rewritten or dropped.
  • A plan with themes, so topics rotate on purpose instead of by accident.

In practice: every post we publish is logged in one sheet with its date and link, so the record exists before the next draft is written.

4. It posts at the wrong moment

Why it happens. Scheduled posts are written days ahead. A cheerful promotion the morning after a complaint, during a closure or on a day of bad local news reads badly. A system only knows what you tell it.

What fixes it.

  • One switch to stop everything. Each channel needs a pause you can use from your phone, without logging in to a tool.
  • Approval close to the post date, not weeks ahead, at least in the first months.
  • A short do-not-post list: the days and situations when nothing goes out, such as days you are closed, an open complaint or a crisis in your area.
  • Replies stay human. Complaints and unusual questions always come to you.

In practice: each of our channels has its own switch, and only the exact word “live” lets a post go out. Anything else means nothing is published.

There is a fifth worry: that people, or Google, will be able to tell. Here is what the platforms themselves say.

Do Google and Meta Penalize AI-Written Posts?

Not for being written with AI. In its guidance on AI-generated content (February 8, 2023), Google Search says its ranking systems aim to reward original, high-quality content, and that it focuses on the quality of content rather than how it is produced. What breaks its spam policies is using automation, AI included, mainly to manipulate search rankings. In Google’s own words about AI content: “It’s just content.”

Meta labels AI-generated video, audio and images on Facebook, Instagram and Threads. Since July 2024 the label has been called “AI info”, and it is based on industry-standard signals in the file or on people disclosing it themselves. Since September 2024, content that was only edited with AI tools shows the label in the post’s menu instead of on the post. Meta’s announcement covers video, audio and images; it does not describe labels for text.

So the words in your post are judged like any other words. The pictures are where an AI label can appear, which is one more reason to use real photos.

The question is not whether AI can write. It is what it is allowed to say without you.

What Runs for Us Today

Every article on this blog and every post on our own channels goes through the same path: written from sources, checked, held for approval, then published. Our news publication has run this way for months. Anything the system cannot verify is held back and shown to a person instead of being sent.

Some of our customers now schedule their social media through the same automation. They approve, and the system publishes. When we connected our own Facebook page, we limited the connection to that one page, so it cannot post anywhere else, even by mistake.

What Can We Learn From This?

1. The risk is facts, not tone. Spend your setup time on a short facts file, not on making the AI sound like you. A post with the right facts in plain words is never embarrassing.

2. Approval is a phase, not a life sentence. Read every post for the first few weeks. Once a type of post has gone right many times, it can go out on its own while anything new still comes to you.

3. Real photos beat generated ones. A real photo of your work or your place builds trust, and it never carries an AI label.

How Practical Is This in Real Life?

Very, with honest limits. The setup needs you once: what you offer and what you don’t, the prices you are happy to publish, the areas you serve, and the words you never want used. After that, your part is a quick yes on your phone for each post.

The limits are real. It does not know what happened in your business today unless you tell it, so you need a simple way to pause a post. It cannot photograph a job nobody photographed. And if a tool promises it runs itself from day one with no review, ask what checks a post before it is published.

A Checklist Before You Let AI Post for You

  • Write a one-page facts file: services, prices you publish, areas you serve, what you don’t do.
  • List the words and claims that are off-limits.
  • Decide who approves, and make the yes a tap on your phone.
  • Make sure the tool can reach only your own page.
  • Ask what happens when it cannot verify something: it should hold the post, not guess.
  • Use your own photos or real stock photos, never generated people presented as staff or customers.

With those six in place, AI writes the routine posts and you keep the final word.
